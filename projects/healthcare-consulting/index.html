The challenge

People seeking health coverage kept losing the thread.

A major public health benefits portal saw high volumes of visitors seeking coverage and eligibility information — many of whom never found a clear path to it.

1

Many visitors arrived specifically seeking coverage or eligibility information.

2

Key calls-to-action underperformed relative to that intent.

3

Navigation and content discoverability issues led to abandonment or over-reliance on search.

4

Content readability was mismatched with the literacy levels of the target audience — often people navigating government systems under time pressure or with varying health literacy.

◆These barriers delayed access to essential health coverage for vulnerable populations — which is what made findability a health-equity problem, not just a UX one.
My role & methods

Mixed methods, centered on what people actually do.

As a Usability Analyst, I led behavioral UX research on the client's public benefits platform and delivered prioritized, actionable recommendations directly to their team.

Method 01

Session replay & heatmaps

Reviewed how visitors actually interacted with critical pages — homepage, navigation, resource and search results — revealing scrolling patterns, hesitation, and navigation struggles in real time.

Method 02

Survey data synthesis

Analyzed responses from recent site visitors seeking coverage information, prioritizing negative satisfaction signals, visit reasons, and open-ended comments about findability.

Method 03

Prioritization framework

Ranked findings by negative feedback volume, coverage-seeking intent, and site information-quality metrics to focus recommendations where they'd matter most.

◆Why these methods: session replays and heatmaps show what users actually do, not just what they report — critical for high-stakes public services where people face cognitive load, stress, or time constraints. Survey comments then supplied the why.
Key insights

Four barriers, found in the behavior — not just the feedback.

Each insight is grounded in what people did on the page, then confirmed by what they said about it afterward.

01

Primary CTA lacked prominence

No button styling or hover feedback, sometimes below the fold. Many coverage-seekers scrolled past it without recognizing it as interactive.

02

Complex megamenu navigation

Visitors hovered over global navigation without selecting, then switched to search — the menu was too broad or deep for quick access.

03

Buried pagination

On resource pages, few visitors scrolled far enough to reach pagination controls, so they never discovered additional valuable content.

04

Readability & health literacy gap

Open-ended comments repeatedly flagged content as overly complex — confirmed by the formal readability analysis and visitor quotes below.

04 Insight 04 · In depth Readability & health literacy gap
19

My readability analysis showed that readers needed 19 years of formal education to comprehend key eligibility pages — several years of post-secondary schooling.

What visitors actually said

Said the site needed layman's terms — that you'd need a PhD to follow the nuances in the wording.
Couldn't find a clear, direct answer to a simple question about eligibility requirements.
Asked for simple terms that most people could actually understand.
Wanted the site made simple, easy to understand, and easy to navigate.
Recommendations

Five priorities, focused on visibility, simplicity, and inclusion.

Delivered directly to the client as a prioritized, feasible set of changes — not a wish list. The priorities below are from this report; in follow-on work across the same engagement, I also recommended sidebar subcategory navigation to progressively disclose content on deeper pages.

Redesign the primary CTA(s) with conventional button styling, strong contrast, hover states, and higher placement — including brief scope notes explaining each pathway.

Test variations of CTA wording, comparing common user terms like "enroll" against existing site language.

Simplify the mega menu by consolidating links and reducing the number of categories.

Add pagination and "items per page" controls at both the top and bottom of resource lists.

Systematically simplify on-page language to plain-language standards, using readability tools to validate and iterate.

  • Write for a general audience, not subject-matter experts.
  • Target a 4th- to 7th-grade reading level, guided by established readability tools.
  • Favor shorter sentences, common words, clear structure, and direct answers to common questions.

Homepage CTA improvement

Before

Low-prominence text link

A plain text link sat low on the page with significant unused hero space — easy to scroll past entirely.

After

High-contrast CTA + scope note

Conceptual redesign with standard button styling, user-centered wording, and a supporting scope note near the hero.

Simplified navigation structure

Before

Dense, six-category megamenu

Visitors hovered across many options without selecting, then abandoned the menu for search — a sign it was too broad for a high-intent task.

After

Task-oriented, consolidated groups

Categories consolidated into get coverage, manage benefits, and help — reduced link density, stronger hierarchy and hover feedback.

Why this mattered: the redesigned menu surfaces plain-language labels that match how people actually search for enrollment pathways, not internal site taxonomy.
  • Consolidates six subcategories into three task-oriented groups.
  • Reduces peer-link density and strengthens visual hierarchy.
  • The interactive prototype below illustrates one approach to this report's megamenu recommendation — not the follow-on sidebar subcategory navigation recommended later in the engagement.

Resource pages with dual pagination

Before

Bottom-only pagination

Visitors had to scroll past the entire list before moving to the next page — friction under time pressure or with assistive technology.

After

Top-and-bottom controls

Pagination at both ends, with items-per-page selection, numbered pages, and a jump-to-last option.

Outcomes & impact

What actually shipped after the engagement.

The analysis gave the team evidence-based priorities. Here's what happened after my involvement ended.

Implemented

The client decided to simplify the mega menu by implementing progressive disclosure to visually declutter the complex navigation structure. This is explored in the interactive prototype.

Implemented

Sidebar subcategory navigation on some pages — consistent with a follow-on recommendation from later in the engagement, though not part of this report.

Partial

Primary coverage-related links updated to conventional link styling (color + underline) with hover states, though still without distinct button treatment.

Not implemented

Dual pagination — resource lists remain paginated at the bottom only.

Client response

During the presentation, the client expressed strong interest in the findings and treated several recommendations as high-priority next steps.

◆These later changes suggest the research helped surface usability issues the organization went on to address, at least in part.
Reflections

What this project reinforced about public-sector UX.

01

Behavioral data is irreplaceable

For uncovering hidden friction points that self-reported feedback alone would miss.

02

Health literacy is foundational accessibility

Complex language creates real barriers to care, not just inconvenience.

03

Small details scale dramatically

In high-intent, high-stakes flows, a missing hover state has outsized consequences.

04

Public benefits UX has human stakes

Reinforced the real impact of design decisions on vulnerable populations.
