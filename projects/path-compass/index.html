Project Overview

“How might we”

How might we support high-fidelity Trauma-Focused Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (TF-CBT) delivery in community clinics while giving youth greater agency over their information and reducing documentation burden for clinicians and caregivers?

Design focus

A HIPAA-aware clinical system that keeps PRACTICE fidelity, longitudinal outcomes, and trauma-informed privacy in one workflow.

  • Fidelity

    Session-level PRACTICE checklist

  • Outcomes

    Outcome measures across five timepoints

  • Privacy

    Trauma-informed consent & control

Evaluation context

Path Compass is a speculative UX project grounded in a master’s-level program evaluation for Wayne State University’s M.S. in Community Health Education (Measurement and Evaluation Course).

  • 300 youth ages 10–18 with post-traumatic stress symptoms
  • TF-CBT vs Therapy as Usual (TAU) compared in community outpatient clinics
  • 5 timepoints plus ongoing PRACTICE fidelity monitoring

Outcome measurement used the TF-CBT PRACTICE Fidelity Checklist (Deblinger et al., 2014) alongside longitudinal collection with the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ) (Goodman et al., 1998) and the Duke-UNC Functional Social Support Questionnaire (FSSQ) (Broadhead et al., 1988).

This case study translates that framework into user-centered design—how systems-level tooling could support high-fidelity TF-CBT delivery and outcome measurement in real-world clinics.

Design Process

Path Compass follows a five-phase process that moves from the evaluation plan into trauma-informed product design—with research synthesis, competitive pattern analysis, and journey mapping as the primary discovery methods.

  1. 01 Foundation & Research

    Grounded the work in the existing TF-CBT Evaluation Plan and clinical constraints in community outpatient settings.

    • ◆Study design, SDQ/FSSQ instruments, five timepoints, and PRACTICE fidelity requirements
    • ◆Trauma-informed digital design literature review
    • ◆Early non-negotiables: HIPAA-aware workflows, youth agency over disclosure, and documentation that keeps pace with session care
  2. 02 Synthesis & Insight Generation

    Translated the evaluation plan into three research-grounded personas—youth, clinician, and caregiver—and mapped Current State vs Ideal State journeys across evaluation phases.

    • ◆Friction surfaced: documentation burden, privacy risk, dropout pressure, and fragmented outcome tracking
    • ◆Design priorities: live PRACTICE fidelity, automated multi-timepoint surveys, and trauma-informed privacy controls
  3. 03 Exploration & Benchmarking

    Benchmarked adjacent tools against the jobs Path Compass must cover. No single product owns that stack—pattern comparisons clarified the product wedge.

    • ◆Real-time PRACTICE fidelity monitoring
    • ◆Five-timepoint SDQ/FSSQ collection for youth and caregivers
    • ◆Multi-party, trauma-informed privacy
    • ◆Compared against paper checklists, TF-CBT training platforms, in-session toolkits, and measurement-based care systems
  4. 04 Design & Prototyping

    Translated research insights and trauma-informed principles into wireframes, high-fidelity mockups, and interactive prototypes for youth and clinician surfaces.

    • ◆Two wireframes, four mockups (two youth mobile, two clinician desktop), and two prototypes
    • ◆Design decisions grounded in journeys, personas, and competitive pattern review
  5. 05 Evaluation & Validation

    Validation centers on heuristic evaluation rather than primary research with clinic participants, given project scope.

    • ◆Heuristic evaluation against Nielsen’s usability heuristics and trauma-informed digital principles
    • ◆Youth mobile and clinician desktop prototypes reviewed across check-in, privacy, fidelity, and dashboard flows
    • ◆Findings grouped by severity and translated into prototype refinements before any future live evaluation

Path Compass is a conceptual / speculative UX case study grounded in an existing mixed-methods TF-CBT Evaluation Plan. Because of project scope, validation centers on heuristic evaluation rather than primary generative or evaluative user research with clinic participants.

01 Foundation & Research

Before designing screens, the evaluation plan and clinical model set the problem boundary: what fidelity, measures, and TF-CBT structure the product must support.

Evaluation Context

A randomized design assigns 150 youth to TF-CBT and 150 to TAU.

  • Primary measures

    Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ) Emotional Symptoms subscale (worries, fears, emotion regulation) and the Duke-UNC Functional Social Support Questionnaire (FSSQ) (Broadhead et al., 1988; as used in Birkeland et al., 2020), plus eight custom items.

  • Five timepoints

    SDQ and FSSQ from youth and caregivers at baseline, mid-intervention (session 6), post-intervention (sessions 12–15), 12-month follow-up, and 18-month follow-up.

  • Process & analysis

    TF-CBT Fidelity Checklist for PRACTICE adherence. Planned analyses: repeated-measures ANOVA, ANCOVA, mixed-effects models, descriptive fidelity stats, and qualitative theme analysis.

Clinical Framework — TF-CBT PRACTICE

TF-CBT is delivered through the PRACTICE model. Learn more: NCTSN How to Implement TF-CBT guide and a research review of the PRACTICE components.

  • Psychoeducation & parenting skills Normalize trauma reactions and equip caregivers with supportive strategies.
  • Relaxation Build body-based calming skills youth can use when distress rises.
  • Affective modulation Identify, name, and regulate difficult feelings in safer ways.
  • Cognitive coping Notice unhelpful thoughts and practice more balanced alternatives.
  • Trauma narrative Process the trauma story gradually to reduce avoidance and distress.
  • In vivo mastery Face safe reminders of trauma in real life to restore a sense of control.
  • Conjoint child–parent sessions Share progress together and strengthen trust, communication, and support.
  • Enhancing safety & future development Plan for ongoing safety and healthy next steps after treatment ends.

Evaluation Questions

Path Compass was designed to directly support the four core evaluation questions from the TF-CBT Evaluation Plan:

  1. 1
    Process Evaluation (Fidelity)

    To what extent was the TF-CBT intervention implemented with fidelity to the core PRACTICE components in community clinics?

  2. 2
    Short-term Impact

    Are there measurable reductions in internalizing symptoms (worries, fears, and emotion regulation difficulties) as a result of the intervention? (measured via SDQ Emotional Symptoms subscale)

  3. 3
    Intermediate-term Impact

    Are there measurable improvements in perceived social support (measured via the Duke-UNC Functional Social Support Questionnaire – FSSQ) and coping compared to Therapy as Usual (TAU)?

  4. 4
    Long-term Outcome

    Are there broad improvements in mental health outcomes and quality of life in this population 12–18 months post-intervention compared to TAU?

Goals & Expected Outcomes

The evaluation targets measurable gains across fidelity and outcomes in community settings.

  • High-fidelity delivery

    Sustain PRACTICE adherence in real-world community clinics—not just research sites.

  • Symptom & support gains

    Short-term reductions in internalizing symptoms; intermediate FSSQ and coping gains vs. TAU; longer-term mental health and quality-of-life improvements.

  • Projected durability

    Statistically significant symptom reduction, with sustained improvements of at least 45% in internalizing symptoms and 40% in social support at 5–10 years post-intervention.

  • Platform translation

    Embed fidelity monitoring, multi-timepoint data collection, and outcome visualization into everyday TF-CBT workflows—especially where paper checklists and fragmented tools fail.

02 Synthesis & Insight Generation

Personas and journey maps convert evaluation requirements into lived experience—where fidelity, privacy, and longitudinal data collection collide in real clinic life.

Personas

Who they represent

Three research-grounded personas translate the evaluation plan into concrete design decisions:

  • Nia (youth), Marcus (clinician), and Denise (caregiver) mapped to sample, instruments, and five timepoints
  • Nia and Denise form a matched youth–caregiver dyad; Marcus is their clinician
  • Each profile covers demographics, clinical context, goals, frustrations, and platform role

Why Personas?

  • Ground abstract evaluation requirements in lived clinic experience
  • Keep youth app, clinician dashboard, and caregiver portal needs distinct before wireframes harden
  • Pressure-test flows against real constraints—privacy, documentation load, access, and engagement

Trauma-informed lens

  • Safety, choice, trustworthiness, collaboration, and empowerment stay front and center
  • Highlighted chips in each profile flag which principles matter most for that persona
  • Fidelity monitoring and outcome tracking should reduce burden, not add harm

Demographics

She/her, lives in a mid-sized Southeastern Michigan community with her mother, Denise, and younger brother. Denise works two part-time retail jobs; household near the poverty line. Attends public middle school; unstable home Wi-Fi, reliable phone data.

Trauma & Clinical Profile

Exposure to domestic violence and psychological abuse at home. Screens in the abnormal band (7–10) on the SDQ Emotional Symptoms subscale. No prior therapy. Low baseline perceived social support, consistent with the low-support pattern seen in youth entering trauma therapy with high symptom levels.

Goals & Motivations

  • Stop feeling on-edge at home
  • Keep friendships that don’t require “the whole story”
  • Have real control over what she shares, and when

Frustrations & Pain Points

  • Paper mood forms feel like homework and go missing
  • Repeating her story to new adults
  • Fear Denise will see private answers

Tech Behaviors & Access

Personal smartphone is her primary device. Moderate-to-high digital literacy; prefers texting and apps over calls; wary of anything that feels like it’s “reporting” on her.

How Path Compass Helps

Youth check-ins replace paper SDQ forms. Granular sharing controls show exactly what Denise and her therapist can see. Progress view is tied to her own goals, not clinic metrics.

Design Implications

  • 01 Default all narrative/journal entries to private; require an explicit, reversible opt-in to share with Denise.
  • 02 Use warm, non-clinical microcopy — “check in,” not “assessment.”
  • 03 Never auto-notify Denise of a symptom score without Nia’s awareness.
  • 04 Offer a low-stakes preview of any check-in before it “counts,” to reduce first-use anxiety.

“If I have to choose between finishing a fidelity form and actually being present with a kid mid-narrative, I choose the kid every time.”

Trauma-informed care needs emphasized

Safety Choice Trustworthiness Collaboration Empowerment

Demographics

Licensed clinical social worker at a community outpatient mental health clinic. Caseload of 30+, roughly a third trauma-focused. Limited admin support; uses a personal laptop due to outdated clinic hardware.

Clinical Context

Trained in the TF-CBT PRACTICE model — Psychoeducation and parenting skills, Relaxation, Affective modulation, Cognitive coping, Trauma narrative, In vivo mastery, Conjoint sessions, and Enhancing safety — delivered across roughly 12–15 sessions in three near-equal phases.

Goals & Motivations

  • Deliver every PRACTICE component with fidelity
  • Keep families engaged through the full course of treatment
  • Document efficiently enough to still have time with clients

Frustrations & Pain Points

  • Paper fidelity checklists completed after the fact lose accuracy
  • Caregiver no-shows disrupt the parallel-session structure
  • No easy view of a client’s trend across five research timepoints
  • Full PRACTICE-model fidelity is genuinely hard under high caseloads and limited resources, especially when caregivers face their own barriers like poverty, mental illness, or substance use
  • Real-world community outcomes run more modest than tightly controlled efficacy trials — hard to explain to families expecting fast results

Tech Behaviors & Access

Comfortable with EHR-style software; moderate patience for clunky systems. Values anything that reduces documentation time without adding clicks.

How Path Compass Helps

In-session fidelity checklist mirrors the PRACTICE sequence in real time. Dashboard flags missed caregiver-participation thresholds and auto-populates the longitudinal symptom and support data across all five assessment points.

Design Implications

  • 01 Structure the fidelity checklist by PRACTICE phase, markable during the session rather than reconstructed afterward.
  • 02 Surface caregiver-participation status prominently — parallel caregiver sessions are integral, not optional.
  • 03 Provide one longitudinal symptom/support trendline per client spanning all five timepoints.
  • 04 Minimize documentation friction — every extra click competes directly with session time.
  • 05 Frame outcome dashboards against real-world community benchmarks, not idealized efficacy-trial curves, so clinicians and families aren’t measured against an unrealistic bar.

03 Exploration & Benchmarking

Competitive analysis pressure-tests the journey findings against tools clinics already touch—and names the incomplete clusters Path Compass is designed to fill.

Competitive Benchmarking

After mapping Nia and Marcus’s journeys, I benchmarked adjacent tools against the jobs Path Compass must cover:

  • Real-time PRACTICE fidelity during sessions
  • Five-timepoint SDQ and FSSQ collection from youth and caregivers
  • Trauma-informed privacy across youth, caregiver, and clinician surfaces
  • Community-clinic documentation load without competing with care

No single product owns that stack—so the comparison clarifies Path Compass’s wedge rather than chasing EHR feature parity.

Why benchmark?

  • Pressure-test journey findings against TF-CBT training, in-session engagement, measurement-based care, and AI quality improvement tools clinics already use
  • Name incomplete clusters in the landscape before design commitments harden
  • Protect priorities that would otherwise get diluted by “just use our existing EHR” or “add another PROM tool”

Shortlist

  • TF-CBT / PRACTICE-aligned delivery or training
  • Common in community behavioral-health quality improvement
  • Realistic artifact Marcus would already touch in practice

Four Incomplete Clusters

These four clusters represent the current market gaps that Path Compass is designed to address:

  1. 1
    Train & Certify

    TF-CBTWeb and similar platforms effectively teach the PRACTICE model, but they do not support real-world delivery workflows, fidelity logging during sessions, or longitudinal evaluation in community settings.

  2. 2
    Engage in Session

    Tools like SPARK help structure in-session activities and support fidelity in the moment, but they do not provide multi-timepoint outcome tracking or serve as a shared workspace across youth, caregivers, and clinicians.

  3. 3
    Measure Broadly

    Greenspace and EHR measurement-based care add-ons excel at collecting and trending patient-reported outcomes, but they are not built around TF-CBT fidelity monitoring or trauma-informed privacy and narrative-sharing controls.

  4. 4
    Score & Supervise with AI

    Platforms like Lyssn use AI to score session quality and support supervision at scale. However, they function primarily as organizational quality tools rather than collaborative workspaces for youth, caregivers, and clinicians.

Path Compass’s wedge

A clinic workspace that joins PRACTICE fidelity, five-timepoint SDQ/FSSQ collection, and trauma-informed multi-party privacy in one continuum—the gap the journeys hit when paper and fragmented tools collide.

Low-Fidelity Explorations

What I experimented with

Before high-fidelity design, I experimented with layout and interaction patterns for two key screens in low fidelity—checking each direction against trauma-informed digital design literature and the TF-CBT evaluation framework, not live user sessions:

  • Youth check-in — brief self-report at five TF-CBT evaluation timepoints (Goodman et al., 1998), with trauma-informed safety requirements for participants like Nia (14) (Abdulai et al., 2023)
  • Clinician fidelity checklist — in-session PRACTICE component tracking aligned to the TF-CBT Brief Practice Fidelity Checklist (Deblinger et al., 2014)

Youth check-in screen

  • Mood-first sequencing drew on affective modulation as a lower-stakes entry before clinical measures (Cohen & Mannarino, 2015) and trauma-informed safety and choice principles (Abdulai et al., 2023)
  • Discrete response options mirrored validated instruments like the SDQ (Goodman et al., 1998) and limited over-disclosure vs. open text—in line with choice and empowerment (Abdulai et al., 2023)
  • Privacy controls needed visible placement upfront; footnote-style messaging conflicted with trustworthiness and choice in digital trauma-informed care (Abdulai et al., 2023)

Clinician fidelity checklist

  • A single always-visible PRACTICE list aligned with the TF-CBT fidelity checklist structure (Deblinger et al., 2014) and community delivery patterns where post-session recall degrades accuracy (Webb et al., 2014)
  • Expandable inline note rows preserved in-session presence better than modal entry—reducing the reconstruction burden described in community fidelity work (Webb et al., 2014)

Carried into high fidelity

  • Mood-first sequencing and discrete response options retained, grounded in trauma-informed choice (Abdulai et al., 2023) and SDQ-style self-report (Goodman et al., 1998)
  • Privacy controls surfaced prominently rather than buried (Abdulai et al., 2023)
  • Fidelity checklist structured as a single scannable list for real-time marking (Deblinger et al., 2014)

Checked against published trauma-informed and TF-CBT literature rather than clinic participants, these explorations pointed toward interactions that prioritize psychological safety for youth and reduce documentation burden for clinicians.

04 Design & Prototyping

Research surfaced three pressure points. Trauma-informed principles—safety, choice, trustworthiness, collaboration, empowerment—set the bar: visible controls, explicit sharing, and documentation that does not compete with care.

Pressure points

  1. Youth anxiety and over-disclosure risk at first use
  2. Privacy risk when caregiver visibility is the default
  3. Clinicians deferring fidelity paperwork until after session, then reconstructing adherence from memory

Carried into design

  1. Mood-first check-ins with discrete choices and a persistent Private signal
  2. Private-by-default privacy with granular, reversible share per audience
  3. Live PRACTICE fidelity checklist with collapsed notes, plus a clinician dashboard for fidelity, PROM completion, and alerts

Refinements from evaluation

Following the heuristic review, I translated the feedback into targeted design decisions—implemented in the youth prototype and planned for the clinician prototype below.

Youth · Mobile

  • User control and freedom · Trauma-informed Safety / Empowerment

    Built an exit path into check-in

    So youth can pause mid-flow without feeling trapped or forced to finish disclosure.

  • User control and freedom · Error prevention

    Linked Privacy & Sharing back to home

    So privacy controls feel reversible and youth can return to the app without a dead end.

  • Aesthetic and minimalist design · Recognition rather than recall

    Condensed measure prompts to a two-word maximum

    To reduce visual crowding and make each screen easier to scan on a small display.

  • Visibility of system status · Error prevention

    Connected three of four bottom-nav tabs

    Home, Check-in, and My Space are linked so the youth prototype reads as one coherent walkthrough, not isolated screens.

Clinician · Desktop

  • Visibility of system status · Recognition rather than recall

    Add a persistent PRACTICE progress indicator

    So clinicians can see remaining components at a glance instead of counting down during a live session.

  • Error prevention · Trauma-informed Safety / Trustworthiness

    Label and collapse the shared youth check-in panel

    So shared youth signals stay clearly scoped and clinicians can reduce visual noise when the panel isn’t needed mid-session.

  • User control and freedom · Error prevention

    Require confirmation before End Session

    So accidental closure is less likely and clinicians see a summary of what will be recorded before the session closes.

  • Flexibility and efficiency of use · Visibility of system status

    Linked the fidelity checklist to the dashboard

    So reviewers can follow marking in session through to caseload context in one end-to-end flow.

Design strengths from the same evaluation are in Evaluation & Validation.

05 Evaluation & Validation

This section documents how I evaluated the Path Compass prototypes and what I learned—the method, scope, and findings. Because this is a conceptual case study grounded in an existing evaluation plan, validation centered on heuristic evaluation rather than primary research with clinic participants.

Evaluation method

Heuristic evaluation

I conducted a structured heuristic evaluation of the youth and clinician flows using Nielsen’s 10 usability heuristics, layered with trauma-informed digital design principles from the scoping literature on safety, trustworthiness, choice, collaboration, and empowerment.

  • Safety
  • Trustworthiness
  • Choice
  • Collaboration
  • Empowerment

Scope

Review covered the interactive prototypes and their supporting high-fidelity screens across both product surfaces.

Youth · Mobile

  • Check-in flow (mood entry through clinical measure prompts)
  • Privacy & Sharing controls and share/revoke patterns
  • Bottom navigation linking home, check-in, progress, and settings

Clinician · Desktop

  • In-session PRACTICE fidelity checklist and live marking
  • Clinician dashboard (fidelity summary, PROM completion, alerts)
  • Prototype linkage between checklist and dashboard

Key findings

Findings are grouped by prototype and severity—strengths to preserve, higher-priority issues, and lower-priority polish items. Issue findings note which Nielsen heuristic or trauma-informed principle each one violates.

Youth · Mobile

  • Strengths

    • ◆Privacy-first ordering surfaces control cues before sensitive questions, reinforcing agency before disclosure
    • ◆Mood-first sequencing opens with a low-stakes check-in before clinical measures
    • ◆Discrete response options use tappable choices instead of open text, reducing pressure to overshare
    • ◆Share and Revoke carry equal visual weight, so revoking access feels as legitimate as granting it

  • Higher priority

    • ◆

      User control and freedom · Trauma-informed Safety / Empowerment

      No exit path during check-in left youth unable to pause or leave mid-flow without feeling trapped.

    • ◆

      User control and freedom · Error prevention

      Missing back navigation on Privacy & Sharing made the screen feel like a dead end, not a reversible choice.

  • Lower priority

    • ◆

      Aesthetic and minimalist design · Recognition rather than recall

      Measure screens crowd too many scale options into limited mobile space, increasing scan effort.

    • ◆

      Visibility of system status · Error prevention

      Unfinished bottom-nav tabs need empty states that explain what’s coming, not blank dead ends.

Clinician · Desktop

  • Strengths

    • ◆Always-visible PRACTICE list supports real-time marking without context switching mid-session
    • ◆Marking and note-taking are separated into distinct controls, reducing cognitive load
    • ◆Youth check-in panel shows only what Nia has explicitly shared, protecting privacy
    • ◆Real-time feedback loop updates the dashboard when a session ends, closing the caseload view

  • Higher priority

    • ◆

      Visibility of system status · Recognition rather than recall

      No persistent progress indicator left clinicians counting remaining PRACTICE components during live sessions—easy to lose track of what still needs attention while also monitoring youth affect.

    • ◆

      Error prevention · Trauma-informed Safety / Trustworthiness

      Shared youth check-in panel lacked clear labeling and a collapse option, risking visual noise and accidental over-exposure despite showing only what Nia chose to share.

    • ◆

      User control and freedom · Error prevention

      “End Session” had no confirmation step, creating risk of accidental closure without a summary of what would be recorded or sent to the dashboard.

  • Lower priority

    • ◆

      Aesthetic and minimalist design · Recognition rather than recall

      Expanded notes can reduce scannability of the PRACTICE list when multiple rows are open.

    • ◆

      Error prevention · Visibility of system status

      Incomplete navigation targets need empty states or disabled controls so the prototype does not dead-end.

    • ◆

      Visibility of system status

      Dashboard “Updated” state could signal the change more strongly on first return from a session.

How findings informed the final design

Youth findings targeted agency and reversible navigation; clinician findings surfaced session-flow risks, youth-data visibility, and dashboard feedback. Higher-priority issues in both prototypes are captured as design decisions in Refinements from evaluation; clinician strengths informed what to preserve alongside checklist-to-dashboard linkage.
