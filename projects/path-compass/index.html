A guide for youth and clinicians on the path to healing.

From evaluation plan to user-centered design — supporting high-fidelity trauma-focused care through guided progress in community mental health.

Project Overview

“How might we”

How might we support high-fidelity Trauma-Focused Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (TF-CBT) delivery in community clinics while giving youth greater agency over their information and reducing documentation burden for clinicians and caregivers?

Design focus

A HIPAA-aware clinical system that keeps PRACTICE fidelity, longitudinal outcomes, and trauma-informed privacy in one workflow.

  • Fidelity

    Session-level PRACTICE checklist

  • Outcomes

    Outcome measures across five timepoints

  • Privacy

    Trauma-informed consent & control

Path Compass translates a master’s-level TF-CBT evaluation plan into trauma-informed product design—connecting PRACTICE fidelity, five-timepoint outcome measurement, and youth agency over disclosure in community outpatient settings.

01 Foundation & Research

A master’s-level TF-CBT evaluation plan defines what Path Compass must support: PRACTICE fidelity, Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ) and Duke-UNC Functional Social Support Questionnaire (FSSQ) (standard symptom and support questionnaires) at five timepoints, and outcome measurement in community clinics.

Evaluation overview

300 youth (ages 10–18) · TF-CBT vs. Therapy as Usual (TAU) · community outpatient settings

  • SDQ + FSSQ youth and caregiver reports at five timepoints
  • PRACTICE fidelity session-level adherence monitoring
  • 4 questions fidelity through long-term outcomes

Constraints that shaped the design

  • Conceptual case study — no primary research with clinic participants
  • HIPAA-aware workflows; no real patient data
  • Trauma-informed defaults: youth agency, reversible sharing, documentation that does not compete with care
  • Community-clinic caseloads — fidelity and PROM collection must fit real session workflows

TF-CBT PRACTICE components

TF-CBT is delivered through the PRACTICE model—components (the core sequence of TF-CBT skills) clinicians mark for fidelity during care.

  • Psychoeducation & parenting
  • Relaxation
  • Affective modulation
  • Cognitive coping
  • Trauma narrative
  • In vivo mastery
  • Conjoint sessions
  • Enhancing safety

Source materials that informed the design constraints

  • TF-CBT Brief Practice Fidelity Checklist (Deblinger et al., 2014)
  • NCTSN How to Implement TF-CBT guide
  • TF-CBT effectiveness in a community setting (Webb et al., 2014)

Evaluation questions

These evaluation questions formed the foundation of the evaluation and measurement plan I developed for the Measurement & Evaluation in Community Health Education course at Wayne State University’s College of Education.

  1. Process Evaluation Question: Was TF-CBT delivered with PRACTICE fidelity in community clinics?
  2. Short-term Outcome Evaluation Question: Reductions in internalizing symptoms (SDQ)?
  3. Intermediate-term Outcome Evaluation Question: Gains in social support (FSSQ) and coping vs. TAU?
  4. Long-term Impact Evaluation Question: Mental health and quality-of-life improvements 12–18 months post-intervention?

03 Exploration & Benchmarking

No single product covers in-session PRACTICE fidelity, five-timepoint SDQ/FSSQ collection, and trauma-informed multi-party privacy. Benchmarking six adjacent tools clarified Path Compass’s wedge.

  • Train & certify (TF-CBTWeb) teaches the model but doesn’t run delivery workflows or fidelity logging.
  • Engage in session (SPARK) supports fidelity in the moment but lacks longitudinal tracking and caregiver portal features.
  • Measure broadly (Greenspace) trends PROMs but isn’t built around PRACTICE fidelity or granular youth privacy.
  • Score with AI (Lyssn) supports supervision at scale but isn’t a youth–caregiver–clinician workspace.

04 Design & Prototyping

Three pressure points drove the design: youth over-disclosure risk, default caregiver visibility, and post-session fidelity reconstruction.

  • Mood-first check-ins with discrete choices and a persistent Private signal
  • Default-private sharing with granular, reversible controls per audience
  • Live PRACTICE checklist + clinician dashboard for fidelity, PROMs, and alerts

Low-fidelity wireframes

Research-informed layout experiments for youth check-in and clinician fidelity checklist—checked against trauma-informed literature, not live user sessions. Key Design Decisions

The final designs (shown earlier in Key High-Fidelity Screens) emerged from the following choices—each weighed against a plausible alternative from wireframe exploration or competitive review.

Flat, always-visible PRACTICE checklist

Decision

Show all PRACTICE components in a single flat list, markable during session, with optional inline notes.

Alternative considered

Collapsible sections by PRACTICE phase to reduce visual density.

Why this choice

Clinicians reconstruct fidelity from memory after session; flat list keeps all components glanceable mid-narrative.

Default-private youth entries

Decision

All check-in and journal content starts private; youth explicitly share per category and per audience (caregiver vs clinician).

Alternative considered

Caregiver-visible by default with opt-out, or one-time intake privacy settings.

Why this choice

Parent-visible defaults risk over-disclosure; granular revoke operationalizes trauma-informed choice.

Expandable notes vs. modals

Decision

Optional per-item notes expand inline beneath each PRACTICE row.

Alternative considered

Modal dialog for notes, separating marking from documentation.

Why this choice

Modals force context switching during live sessions. Inline expansion keeps the checklist visible while allowing brief documentation when needed.

Mood-first check-in sequencing

Decision

Open youth check-ins with mood, then discrete school-safety and sleep prompts—with a persistent Private signal before input.

Alternative considered

Lead with full clinical measure screens or open-text journaling.

Why this choice

First-use anxiety and over-disclosure risk; low-stakes mood entry builds trust before clinical prompts.

Scoped youth signals for clinicians

Decision

Show only youth check-in data Nia explicitly shared, in a labeled panel clinicians can collapse mid-session.

Alternative considered

Full youth chart visibility or no youth context on the fidelity screen.

Why this choice

Clinical context without over-exposure; labeled, collapsible panel reduces noise mid-session.

Refinements made based on heuristic evaluation

Six fixes applied after Nielsen heuristic review and trauma-informed principles—each tied to a specific usability issue found in the prototypes.

  • Youth fixes 3

    • H3 · User control and freedom · Safety · Empowerment

      Save & Close button in check-in flow

      Early flows trapped users mid-check-in; Save & Close let youth pause, keep partial answers, and leave without feeling forced to disclose.

    • H3 · User control and freedom · Choice · Trustworthiness

      Back navigation on privacy screen

      Privacy settings felt like a dead end; back navigation restored orientation and made sharing choices feel reversible, not final.

    • H1 · Visibility of system status · Safety · Trustworthiness

      Persistent Private status before check-in input

      Youth could not see whether answers were shared; a visible Private signal before each prompt reinforced that disclosure stays under their control.

  • Clinician fixes 3

    • H1 · Visibility of system status · Collaboration

      PRACTICE progress indicator

      Clinicians could not see where they were in the session arc; a progress indicator surfaced phase and component status without pulling attention from the youth in the room.

    • H6 · Recognition rather than recall · Safety · Trustworthiness

      Labeled, collapsible youth check-in panel

      Shared youth signals were easy to miss or misread; a labeled, collapsible panel made only consented data visible and reduced accidental over-exposure mid-session.

    • H5 · Error prevention · Trustworthiness

      End Session confirmation; dashboard linkage

      Ending a session risked accidental data loss; confirmation plus a clear return path to the dashboard prevented costly mis-taps and preserved session integrity.

05 Evaluation & Validation

Validation for this conceptual case study relied on expert heuristic review—not moderated testing with clinic participants. I reviewed youth and clinician prototypes against Nielsen’s heuristics and trauma-informed digital design principles.

  • Safety
  • Trustworthiness
  • Choice
  • Collaboration
  • Empowerment

Youth · Mobile

  • Strengths

    • ◆ Privacy-first ordering — review aligned with trauma-informed choice; control cues precede sensitive prompts
    • ◆ Mood-first, discrete responses — low cognitive-load entry pattern; closed scales instead of open text
    • ◆ Equal-weight Share and Revoke — consistent affordances support reversible sharing control

  • Higher-priority issues

    • ◆ H3 · User control and freedom · Safety · Empowerment No exit path during multi-step check-in
    • ◆ H3 · User control and freedom · Choice · Trustworthiness No back navigation on privacy screen
    • ◆ H1 · Visibility of system status · Safety · Trustworthiness No visible private-status signal before check-in input

Clinician · Desktop

  • Strengths

    • ◆ Always-visible PRACTICE list — supports in-session marking without hiding unmarked components
    • ◆ Scoped youth check-in panel — surfaces only consented data, aligned with privacy-by-default model
    • ◆ Running fidelity score — session-level adherence visible during marking, supporting recognition over recall without leaving the checklist

  • Higher-priority issues

    • ◆ H1 · Visibility of system status · Collaboration No PRACTICE session progress indicator
    • ◆ H6 · Recognition rather than recall · Safety · Trustworthiness Youth check-in panel lacked clear labeling and collapse control
    • ◆ H5 · Error prevention · Trustworthiness No confirmation before ending session; weak return path to dashboard

Reflections & Lessons Learned

What the project demonstrated, where it fell short, and what would come next in a real clinic setting.

What it achieved

  • ◆ Designed an end-to-end system joining live PRACTICE fidelity, five-timepoint outcomes, and trauma-informed youth privacy
  • ◆ Gave youth granular, reversible control over disclosure instead of default caregiver visibility
  • ◆ Shifted clinician documentation from post-session reconstruction to in-session marking
  • ◆ Delivered interactive prototypes covering both youth mobile and clinician desktop workflows

Decisions that mattered

  • ◆ Used a flat, always-visible PRACTICE list instead of collapsible sections so clinicians can mark fidelity without losing session context
  • ◆ Defaulted all youth entries to private, with per-category and per-audience share/revoke controls
  • ◆ Prioritized in-session fidelity marking over post-session paper forms to protect clinical presence
  • ◆ Sequenced check-ins mood-first with discrete options and a persistent “Private” signal to reduce first-use anxiety

Limitations

  • ◆ Conceptual case study only — no primary research with clinicians or youth
  • ◆ Validation limited to heuristic evaluation; no moderated usability testing
  • ◆ Personas and insights synthesized from literature and the evaluation plan, not field research
  • ◆ No real patient data or live clinic implementation

Next steps

  • ◆ Conduct moderated usability testing with community clinicians and youth (or youth advocates) on the fidelity checklist and privacy controls
  • ◆ Compare documentation time and cognitive load against paper PRACTICE checklists
  • ◆ Test share/revoke flows with matched youth–caregiver dyads to validate trust and comprehension
  • ◆ Explore how fidelity and PROM data could support supervisor or quality-improvement views

Transferable insight

  • ◆ Trauma-informed design is operationalized through interaction defaults and control, not visual style
  • ◆ Privacy in multi-party mental health tools must be granular and revocable at every share point
  • ◆ Documentation that competes with clinical presence is rarely sustainable in practice
  • ◆ In-session fidelity marking is more realistic than post-session reconstruction under real caseload pressure
